On my first year at the uni, I had a task to write a review of some article. I had an article on linguistic norm and how it changed. The author claimed, that the moving force for a norm are the *errors made by the users*.

Since then I can't say someone is wrong when they mispronounce, use wrong grammar or make any other language mistake[^smart-words].

## The world of multiple solutions

Also, you can express your thought in many ways.

1. **Use any of the human languages**. You may not even speak one you use in a case. For example, in a grocery in Lithuania I'd ask a loyalty card by saying 'Galima jusu kortele?' But I don't speak Lithuanian.

2. **System of signs**. This happened in math or with driving signals. When a cyclist raises hand in USA or in Poland it always means stop.

   You can also look at how UX/UI designers use same symbols on any site. 3 dots, home, and other symbols became signs in linguistic sense due to their universal usage.

3. **Draw a scheme**. Which is probably the best if both participants of communication don't have a common language.

If you both speak same language you can apply different words.

- If you speak to a friend, you use lower register and probably more colloquial words.
- If you're speaking to your boss or a committee at your work, you'd like to use strict language, expressing your ideas without emotions[^facts].
- If you're writing a program, you're expected to use logical approaches and very specific syntax used in your programming language.

So you can solve your linguistic task in many ways and several solutions may be a good fit for your case.

It works in other real life spheres where there's no predefined right solution[^real-options]. And the big idea is not that if something works in context A it won't work on context B, but that in both A and B you may use solutions x, y, and z that'll give you same (or different) value and fit.

When defining a solution many of us tend to be logical. Recently I read a book Alchemy by Rory Sutherland who said that sometimes irrational would work better.

I am now looking for a job. The rational solution for a search would be to apply at more open positions. So that at some point the magic of many attempts would get you to an offer. While in fact, applying **less** and using more actions like studying a company, adjusting your CV, etc. would get you an offer faster[^intuitive].

While first part of my text is about 'not following a norm', in fact sometimes a norm is helpful and you may benefit from using it.

## Listening to the norm

Your work has own lingo and a 'norm' of using it[^memes]. If you want to be a part of a collective, you'd better use same language even if it hurts. At my current job we have usual Scrum-like methodology: grooming, planning, and a daily meeting. For some reason we call groomings Three Amigos here[^three-amigos]. As I wanted to fit in, I started to apply more exotic terminology myself.

So the norm is a way to distinguish similar people from some circle from the rest of the world.

You may also benefit from using a norm when building something. Here I'd speak about laws, not just wordings. You may listen to norm (*law* in this case) before starting a business so that you don't get problems.

You may also benefit from a norm when your project is highly regulated. E.g., it involves personal data or must comply with some specific ISO standards.

So sticking with a norm is also a solution.

## Is being wrong even wrong?

Going back to the initial take: "Errors provoke change in language". Without trying you may stay within the norm using conventional approaches that aren't always best optimised for your particular case.

An error ~~that doesn't kill you~~ should be treated as an experiment. And maybe you could invent a new norm for something?
